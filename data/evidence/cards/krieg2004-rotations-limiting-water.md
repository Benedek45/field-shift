## Findings
- In West Texas nearly 5 million acres of cotton are grown annually, with rainfall from 26 inches on the east side of the Rolling Plains to 16 inches on the west side of the High Plains.
- In the Rolling Plains, evaluation of tillage and rotation crops showed that reduced tillage raised cotton yields but the rotation crop had no significant effect on cotton yield; continuous cotton with reduced tillage gave the highest yield and profit.
- On the Southern High Plains winter wheat is not a viable rotation crop because winter precipitation probabilities are very low, while grain sorghum fits the rainfall pattern much better.
- Much dryland acreage on the Southern High Plains is classified highly erodible land, so a residue-producing crop is required on part of the area to keep USDA programme benefits.
- Dryland cotton there is mostly planted in a 2x1 skip-row pattern, and planting cotton solid allows the 33% of land in the blank row to grow grain sorghum, which was evaluated over 10 years.
- Summer rain in the region averages 14 inches, which the authors say can make a reasonable dryland cotton yield.
## Conditions and limits
- An extension conference paper summarising regional experience, not a controlled multi-year experiment; the rotation conclusion is specific to water-limited Texas.
- The Rolling Plains and Southern High Plains behave differently, and the recommendation changes between them.
- Cotton root rot in the central and southern Rolling Plains would need three consecutive years of a grain crop to reduce incidence, which the authors call economically unviable.
