## Findings
- Intensifying wheat-fallow by shortening or removing fallow changes insect, pathogen and weed complexes, favouring some pest groups while disadvantaging others.
- Weeds traditionally controlled during the fallow period may be favoured by intensified cropping, while pests that specialise on wheat should be disadvantaged.
- Crop rotation remains a key avoidance strategy for pathogens and helps broaden herbicide options as fallow is reduced.
- Pest management in intensified systems may put less emphasis on controlling volunteer wheat, which changes the green bridge for wheat pests.
- Chemical control is expected to become more complicated because of drift concerns, crop rotational restrictions, herbicide-tolerant crops and glyphosate-resistant weeds.
## Conditions and limits
- A review of pest biology and management, not a trial: it gives no yield or loss coefficients.
- The authors expect changes to be evolutionary rather than revolutionary, so short-term trials may miss the pest shifts.
- Regional across the Great Plains; the specific pest complex differs between the northern and southern Plains.
## Quotes
- "Certain pest groups, such as weeds, traditionally controlled during the fallow period, may be favored by intensified cropping while others, such as those specializing on wheat, should be disadvantaged."
- "Crop rotation will remain a key avoidance strategy for pathogens and will help broaden herbicide options."
- "Preventive practices in dryland production systems currently emphasize the control of grassy weeds while intensified systems may have less emphasis on the control of volunteer wheat."
